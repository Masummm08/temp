§1The bottleneck

Mathematics is constrained by memory

Working memory holds and manipulates information over short periods: what each variable means, which steps are done, what the goal is. It is remarkably limited, and much of the difficulty in maths comes from preserving partial results while you keep calculating.

In your head
347 × 286 = ? hold 69,400… carry 27,760… wait, what was the first one?

The operations are simple. Holding the pieces is the hard part.

On paper
347× 2862,08227,76069,40099,242

Paper doesn’t make you smarter. It expands your working memory.

How experts cope: chunking

a²+2ab+b² → (a + b)² one object, one slot

A novice sees a string of symbols; an expert sees one familiar structure. Chunking compresses information so more fits. It doesn’t remove the limit.

§2The evidence

Working memory predicts maths beyond IQ

2010 Alloway & Alloway

Six-year longitudinal study. Working memory at age 5 predicted literacy and numeracy at 11, even with IQ in the model, and predicted them more strongly than IQ.

2011 Alloway & Passolunghi

In children, working memory made a distinct contribution to maths skill, separate from verbal ability.

2013 Friso-van den Bos et al.

Meta-analysis of primary-school studies: a consistent link between working memory and maths, varying by task type.

2015 Blankenship et al.

Working memory explained unique variation in maths fluency and calculation after controlling for IQ and age.

Don’t overread it. Working memory and intelligence overlap substantially, statistics can’t cleanly separate them, and none of this means commercial working-memory training will boost IQ or maths.

Among children of similar measured intelligence, the ability to hold and update information still predicts maths performance. If humans are capped by this bottleneck, a machine without it is playing a different game.

§3The notebook

The context window is a gigantic notebook

It isn’t the same as human working memory. It’s closer to a huge external notebook paired with an imperfect system for searching what’s written in it. When a model writes x = 6 and later x + 3 = 9, both stay in context to be attended to again. The text isn’t a report of the reasoning; it is part of the mechanism.

CapacityHuman mindLanguage model
Private, silent stateCan pick a number, hold it, transform it, replace it without writing anything HumanWeak; most stable memory is the tokens already generated
Explicit recordA handful of unfamiliar items unaidedDozens or hundreds of items, kept verbatim AI
UpdatingActive: attention, inhibition, continuous revisionPassive: earlier tokens are fixed; it writes new ones instead
ReliabilityFades, tires, drops intermediate resultsCan overlook or get distracted by details; advertised length ≠ usable memory

Best name for it: augmented symbolic working memory. Weaker inside, vastly bigger outside, and maths happens to live outside.

§4Why maths

Almost everything in a proof can be written down

assumptionsdefinitionsknown equationscurrent objectiveresults provedcases eliminatedvalidity conditions

Once written, it stays put. An integer stays an integer; a strict inequality doesn’t drift into a non-strict one with mood or context. Mathematical symbols are designed to remove ambiguity, which makes maths a near-perfect fit for an intelligence that works through a large text workspace.

The human error isn’t lack of insight. It’s a failure of bookkeeping.

Hard problems often hide one deep insight near the start, followed by a lot of unglamorous work: expanding, checking cases, carrying conditions through. A machine doesn’t need deeper insight to win that part. It just needs to keep the whole state in view.

§5Long chains

Difficulty is also the number of steps

A proof is a chain: A → B → C → D. Someone can follow every local step of a 100-step argument and still be unable to produce it unaided, because the global structure won’t fit in their head. Writing nearly everything down removes that ceiling.

Why more “thinking time” helps: what looks like deeper thought may often be broader search inside a bigger notebook.

§6The contrast

Why the advantage shrinks outside maths

Mathematics

  • PremisesExplicit and complete; derive the consequences
  • ConceptsExact variables with fixed meanings
  • FeedbackSubstitute back, evaluate numerically, test cases in code, verify in a proof assistant
  • Bigger memoryConverts directly into better results

Informal reasoning

  • PremisesHidden causes, facts never observed
  • Concepts“Fair”, “successful”, “harmful”: meanings shift with culture and goals
  • FeedbackWeak or years late; a coherent argument can be completely wrong
  • Bigger memoryHelps, but the real problem is finding the right causal model

“Why has Maria stopped replying?” Years of messages can show changes in tone and timing. They can’t tell you whether she’s angry, busy, ill, or lost her phone.

§7The reframe

Intelligence, or cognitive architecture?

Solver A

Works entirely in their head.

Solver B

Unlimited paper, perfect notes, instant recall of every prior calculation, parallel attempts, and a machine that checks each result.

If B wins, we wouldn’t say B has more raw mathematical intelligence. We’d say B had a better setup for the task. The same caution applies to humans versus AI, whose performance is the product of five factors:

KnowledgeVast training on maths
ReasoningGets the attention: the philosophically exciting part
MemoryMay be doing far more of the work than we realise
SearchMany candidate solutions
VerificationCode, tools, checks
§8Predictions

A hypothesis you can test

many interacting constraintslong calculationsextensive case analysisrepeated reference to earlier resultsexact symbolic bookkeepinglarge bodies of formal definitions
If you shrink the notebook

Cutting usable context, or stopping a model from writing intermediate steps, should hurt long maths tasks disproportionately.

If you give humans one

Clear notation, diagrams, software and structured notes should close part of the gap.

A fairer contest

AI with its tools against a human with equally strong external memory and verification, not a human thinking unaided.

A brilliant mathematician may still win when the challenge is finding a new representation of a problem. Once that representation exists, AI may be better at exploring everything that follows from it.

§9Two geniuses

Von Neumann, amplified. Not yet Einstein.

Eugene Wigner knew both men. He wrote that he never met a mind as “quick and acute” as John von Neumann’s, yet he judged Albert Einstein’s understanding deeper and more original.

Today’s AI looks like this

von Neumann

  • Immense speed
  • Absorbs vast information
  • Follows very complex arguments
  • Moves across fields quickly

+ a practically unlimited notebook

vs.
The missing piece

Einstein

  • Discards the accepted framework
  • Finds the hidden conceptual error
  • Invents a new way of seeing reality

The analogy has limits: von Neumann was himself immensely original, not just a fast calculator. But the distinction captures something real.

The next threshold

When AI can recognise that a problem has been framed incorrectly, and invent a fundamentally better way of understanding it.
